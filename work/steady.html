Case study 01 · Mobile app

Steady — a first entry, not a sign-up wall

New users of a journaling app were asked for an account, a goal and notification settings before they could write a single word. I redesigned onboarding around one idea: let people feel the value first.

01 — The problemPeople came to write. We asked them to fill in forms.

Steady is a (fictional) journaling app. Its onboarding had seven screens: create an account, pick goals, choose a writing time, allow notifications, and only then an empty page.

The brief was to increase the number of new users who complete their first journal entry. My working hypothesis: the value arrives too late, so people leave before they ever experience it.

02 — ResearchTalking to people who stopped journaling

I interviewed six people who had tried a journaling app and given up, reviewed the onboarding of five competing apps, and ran a short survey with 40 respondents about when and why they write.

I just wanted to get one thing out of my head, and it asked me for my goals for the year.

— Interview participant, 27
The psychology behind it

Start people halfway there

BJ Fogg's behavior model says an action happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet. At install, motivation is at its peak, but a long setup drains ability exactly when it matters most.

The endowed progress effect (Nunes & Drèze, 2006) adds that people are more likely to finish something when they feel they have already started. So the first screen should be real progress, not paperwork.

03 — InsightsWhat I learned

  1. The first session is emotional, not administrative. People open the app because something is on their mind right now.
  2. A blank page is intimidating. Five of six participants said a gentle prompt would have helped them start.
  3. Commitment grows after the first entry, not before. People were happy to create an account once they had something worth saving.

04 — IdeationFlipping the order

I mapped the existing flow and sketched three alternatives. The winning direction moved writing to the very first screen and deferred everything else until after the first entry was saved.

05 — Final designWrite first, set up later

The new flow opens straight to a single, friendly prompt. Account creation appears only when the user saves, framed as "keep this entry safe". Reminder settings come last, based on the time the person actually wrote.

06 — TestingTesting with five people

I ran an unmoderated prototype test in Maze with five participants who had never used a journaling app, comparing the old and new flows.

5 / 5

participants wrote a first entry in the new flow (vs. 2 / 5 in the old one)

~40s

median time to the first entry, down from about 3 minutes

4 / 5

chose to create an account after writing

These are small-sample, directional results. They show the idea is worth building, not that it will move retention by a specific amount.

07 — ReflectionWhat I'd do next

If I continued this project I would measure 7-day retention with a live beta, test the prompt copy with a wider range of people, and look closely at accessibility of reminders for people who journal to manage anxiety.

The biggest lesson for me: the order of steps is itself a design decision, and sometimes the best onboarding is almost no onboarding.
