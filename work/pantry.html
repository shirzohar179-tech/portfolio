Pantry — nudging households to waste less

People buy food with good intentions and throw a lot of it away. I designed a grocery companion that helps households use what they have, using defaults and timing instead of guilt.

01 — The problemGood intentions, forgotten vegetables

Pantry is a (fictional) grocery-list app. Users could log what they bought and when it expires, but almost nobody did, so the app could not warn them in time.

The goal: help households use food before it goes bad, without making the app feel like homework.

02 — ResearchA week inside five kitchens

I ran a 7-day diary study with five households, who photographed what they threw away and why, followed by short interviews. I also reviewed four food-tracking apps and their store reviews.

I know there's spinach in there somewhere. I just remember it when it's already slime.

— Diary study participant, parent of two
The psychology behind it

Make the right choice the default

Defaults are powerful: people tend to stick with the pre-selected option (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003). If typing an expiry date is optional, it won't happen, but if a sensible date is already filled in, most people will simply keep it.

Timing matters too. Remembering to do something in the future is fragile, so a reminder works best when it arrives at the moment of the decision, not at a random hour.

03 — InsightsWhat I learned

  1. Nobody enters expiry dates by hand. Every participant skipped that field in existing apps.
  2. Reminders at the wrong moment get ignored. The useful moment is when people decide what to cook.
  3. Guilt doesn't motivate, ideas do. "What can I make with this?" was far more helpful than "this is expiring".

04 — IdeationFrom tracking to suggesting

I explored three concepts: barcode scanning, receipt scanning and auto-suggested shelf life. Auto-suggestion won because it asked the least of people and worked for fresh produce without barcodes.

05 — Final designDefaults, a “use soon” shelf, and dinner-time nudges

Items get a suggested shelf life automatically, which users can adjust with one tap. A "use soon" shelf surfaces what's about to expire, and an optional reminder arrives around the time the household usually plans dinner, paired with a simple recipe idea.

06 — TestingTesting the flow

I tested a clickable prototype with five participants, asking them to add a week's shopping and respond to a reminder.

~4s

5 / 5

3 / 5

These are small-sample, qualitative results from a prototype, not measured behavior change over time.

07 — ReflectionWhat I'd do next

Next, I would validate the suggested shelf-life data with a food-safety source, test how often reminders become annoying over a month, and design for shared households where several people shop.

My takeaway: asking less of people is often the most effective design move.
